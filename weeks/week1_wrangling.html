Join the tables

flights_planes <- flights |>
  left_join(
    planes |> select(tailnum, year_built = year),
    by = "tailnum"
  )

  • flights |> left_join(...) means “start with flights and add columns to it”.

  • planes |> select(tailnum, year_built = year) means “from planes, keep only two columns, and rename year to year_built”. The renaming is what avoids the clash.

  • by = "tailnum" means “match the rows using the tailnum column”.

  • left_join keeps every flight, even if its plane isn’t found in planes. Those flights just get an NA (missing) in year_built.

Calculate plane age and check the join

flights_planes <- flights_planes |>
  mutate(plane_age = 2013 - year_built)

Check the correlation

# A tibble: 1 × 1
  correlation

cor() gives a number between -1 and 1. Values near 0 mean almost no linear relationship. use = "complete.obs" tells R to ignore rows where either value is missing, which is how we skip the flights with unknown plane age or cancelled flights.

0.02 × 0.02 = 0.0004, or about 0.04%

There is a slight correlation (-0.02; 0.04%) between plane age and departure delays, which tells us that there is a negative correlation; newer planes experience greater delays. However, the correlation is very small and indicates almost no linear relationship.

Is 0 inside the interval?

  • 0 is inside (e.g., -0.01 to +0.03): the data are compatible with no relationship at all, so the result is not significant.

  • 0 is outside: zero isn’t a plausible value, so the result is significant.

The correlation between plane age and departure delay was r = -0.015 (95% CI -0.019 to -0.012, p < 0.001). This is statistically significant because of the very large sample, but the effect is negligible: plane age explains about 0.02% of the variation in delays

Reading the ANOVA table

  • Df (2): degrees of freedom. Airport has 2 because there are 3 airports, and it’s always the number of groups minus 1.

  • Sum Sq: the total variation in delays, split into the part linked to airport (1,280,515) and the part left over (529,886,717).

  • F value (396.9): how large the between-airport variation is relative to the within-airport variation. Larger means the group averages differ more than you’d expect by chance.

  • Pr(>F) <2e-16: the p-value. R stops printing at about 2.2e-16, so it shows “less than” that. In a write-up, put p < 0.001.

  • 8255 observations deleted: flights with a missing dep_delay, which are cancelled flights. This is the same missing-data exclusion you wrote down for plane age.

The effect size

R² = 0.0024 means airport explains about 0.24% of the variation in departure delay. You can check it from the table: 1,280,515 / (1,280,515 + 529,886,717) ≈ 0.0024. R² is just the airport row’s share of the total.

The parts of one box

  • The thick line inside the box is the median: the middle value. Half of that airport’s flights were delayed less than this, half more. Note that it’s the median, not the mean, so it won’t match the averages you calculated.

  • The box itself spans the middle 50% of flights, from the 25th percentile (bottom edge) to the 75th percentile (top edge). A taller box means delays vary more from flight to flight. The box height is called the interquartile range (IQR).

  • The whiskers (lines sticking out above and below) extend to the most extreme values that aren’t considered outliers, roughly 1.5 box-heights beyond the box.

  • The dots beyond the whiskers are outliers: unusually large or small delays. Delay data has a huge number of these on the high side, since some flights are delayed for hours.

What to look at when comparing the three boxes

  1. Are the median lines at similar heights? If so, a typical flight has a similar delay at each airport.

  2. Are the boxes similar in height? A taller box means less predictable delays.

  3. Which airport has the longer upper whisker or more dots? That’s where the worst delays cluster.

  4. How much do the boxes overlap vertically? A lot of overlap means the airports are similar, which matches your R² of 0.0024. If the boxes sat clearly apart, airport would matter more.

Why the medians may look odd

Most flights leave close to on time, so the median is often below zero (early) or only a few minutes late, while the mean is pulled higher by the long delays. That’s why the mean and median differ, and it’s the reason Exercise 3 asked you to compare them.
